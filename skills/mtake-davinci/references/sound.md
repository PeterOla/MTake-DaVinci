# Score, narration, captions and sound

## Spot the scene before recording

Build a cue sheet from the story clock. List where the viewer hears a clue, the visible source, what changes, when the shot changes acoustic perspective and what sound survives the cut. Separate dialogue, music and effects at the same frame-zero origin, sample rate and length. Keep a clean recording for each character across the film. Choose a voice through 15–20 second auditions with two different kinds of sentence. Record whole thoughts. Carry timbre, mic perspective, pace and room tone across pickups.

**Narration around beat drops.** Listen to the actual recording at normal speed. Mark the first audible attack of each chosen beat, not the peak at the end of its attack. Mark the actual final consonant/vowel in the preceding line, the first readable picture change, the first audible effect and the first word after it. A waveform helps find the edge; listening resolves a breath, reverb or a stretched automatic word alignment. Pick the frame containing the audio onset. Frame numbers here start at zero. Compute floor(seconds × fps) and inspect the adjacent pictures. Keep sample timing in the stems for the final encode.

In the 30 fps rhythm study, the opening picture accent was on frame 5, with an audible attack near 0.16 s. Frame 41 coincided with a stressed word. Frames 77 and 92 fell inside a single spoken gap, with the preceding word ending around 2.22 s and the next audible word starting around 4.08 s. The original varied its treatment across hits. For a director requesting a clear drop, reserve the entire cluster for score and effects. At script timing, move the setup before the cluster. At recording, shorten or retake a cramped thought. At release, listen to the last consonant, music attack and first returning word in the encoded mix. See the [worked original example](../examples/the-gap/README.md).

**Compose the buildup.** Start tension on the first clue. Choose a musical sound with an identifiable gesture, attack and decay, such as a felt-piano note whose pitch returns with new meaning. Let its level or harmony develop before the event. A riser can grow from a source the audience already hears. A short movement cue can close the remaining distance. Give the main transient a clear front edge, a body that reads on a phone, and a tail whose space belongs to the location. Preserve the music reference level across scene changes. Match related impacts for the dramatic brief while using timbre, reflection and width for perspective. Silence at a deliberate pause or close is a directing choice.

**Cut with sound.** Let the room or mechanical bed continue underneath a picture join. Bring the next acoustic space in early if the camera takes us there. Stop a diegetic source on the exact visual pause click when the story needs an abrupt stop. At an ending, a low receding street sound can carry the last held image. Reserve the final words and the out-point so the viewer can actually read the inscription or card.

**Document what you hear.** Separate rhythmic pulses, sustained notes, voice fundamentals, noise and effects. In a reference, describe a soft keyed or plucked attack, pitch direction, sustain, resonance and decay. Mark instrument identity as inferred unless credits or reliable stems establish it. For a ten-second audit, count distinct effect starts once and list ongoing beds separately. Measure isolated-effect and full-mix loudness on the same boundaries. Distinguish RMS dBFS, integrated LUFS and true peak, and state the meter and window. Digital silence reads as minus infinity; a gated meter may give no result for a very short or quiet window. Cue count measures activity; ask what each audible event actually explains.

**Caption design.** Prefer short thought groups with a stable baseline and enough dwell for a first read. Speak units aloud; print the exact number and unit on the evidence card. Highlight a document passage as it is read. Preview with platform UI margins and the actual background. Deliver a separate subtitle track for accessibility, even when words are drawn into the image. Check a real export for truncation, predictive captions that reveal the answer too soon and blurred fine type.

**Review in three passes.** Solo voice, music and effects. Listen to stereo at 1×. Fold to equal left/right mono and use a small physical speaker if available. A phone-band simulation is a useful additional test. Write what survived, where the score disappeared, what masked the line, and which cue deserves more space. An effects-count target (one production used roughly ten in ten action seconds) applies only where movement and story support it.
