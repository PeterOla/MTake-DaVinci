# Patterns to try on an unfamiliar subject

Choose the one that changes the audience's understanding of this particular scene. The examples below are new applications of decisions observed across ten distinct study cuts and subsequent productions.

| Picture or sound choice | Try it on a different story |
| --- | --- |
| Give materials different marks. | On a station platform, reserve a continuous glint for rail, interrupted grain for timber and a broad fold for a commuter's coat. At phone size each should still read without a label. |
| Spend the strongest accent on an actual discovery. | Hold a modest palette while the meter turns. Give the first contradictory reading the highest contrast or a brief change of colour. A second competing bright sign can steal it. |
| Keep a fixed landmark beside fast motion. | Hold a shelf edge behind a parcel as the conveyor stops. Before exaggerating its slide, check how many parcel widths it moves relative to the shelf. |
| Let evidence cross the edit. | Match a missing roof in the wide view to the same broken beam above the bed. An identical screen direction, edge or sound can make two very different scales belong together. |
| Build a graphic in the order of the argument. | If three readings appear to agree and the fourth breaks the pattern, reveal the first three on a shared scale. Hold the empty fourth position just long enough for someone to predict a value. Add the fourth with its unit. |
| Choose the relationship between picture and impact. | A visible local contact can sound on its exact frame. An unseen source can be heard first and then revealed. A distant scene can show light before the sound arrives. Write the intended relationship in the cue sheet. |
| Movement can supply rhythm under a held note. | On four mechanical actions let the contact sounds carry the pulse, even as a sustained note stays level. Give the last one a longer release if the mechanism has stopped. |
| Separate the work of the voice and the evidence card. | Let the voice explain why one reading matters while the card holds the number, unit, date and source for inspection. Highlight the printed words only as the viewer needs them. |
| Reintroduce a familiar object under new conditions. | Open on a dark station clock, return to the same case in daylight and reveal the missing minute hand. Keep its damage mark and camera orientation recognisable. |
| Vary one musical property while preserving another. | Keep the taps equally spaced as one note rises in pitch. Or keep pitch fixed as gaps between taps shorten. Audition the two versions against the same picture. These choices imply different kinds of tension. |
| Follow through on the object the viewer has adopted. | Stay with an individual wheel until its stamp turns toward the camera; the stamp earns the change of scale. If many things have moved, stay wide long enough to read the shared consequence before choosing a representative detail. |
| Exaggerate the information-bearing shape. | The falling edge of a second sheet can be made clearer by a slightly longer hold or a longer reflected highlight. Keep its thickness, support and contact with the first sheet believable. |

In an unfamiliar film, plan the observation, play the moment without its explanation, and ask a viewer to describe what changed. If they can only repeat the narration, return to the picture or sound that was supposed to carry the discovery.
