# Direct the discovery

Start the cut on a change someone can see or hear. Identify an object, a person or a mark to follow. State privately what the viewer believes at the start and what the last image changes about that belief. A film about shelter can open with an iron object landing in turf. A film about a measuring error can open with two identical-looking readings drifting apart. The second has no need for a dramatic impact.

## Five decisions to make on the shot sheet

| Decision | Observation from development | A check for a different story |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Let the viewer find evidence before naming it. | A detached wheel turned toward the lens. The stamp became readable around 5.27 seconds and was explicitly circled around 14.23. The turn earned the close-up. | In a story about two sheets entering a printer, can someone see the second edge before the narrator says “two”? Record the first readable frame separately from the first spoken word. |
| Connect cause to consequence. | In an early Halifax scene two rooms were exposed while the blocks between them and the waterfront looked untouched. Counting two rooms passed the wrong test. | Draw the wide scene before and after. Hide the favourite detail. What does the rest of the setting say happened? Follow one loose object until its weight is resolved. |
| Give a moving camera something to follow. | The Halifax landing field stayed in sight as streets appeared around it and the view resolved into a map. A previous jump left viewers asking where they were. | Name the fixed landmark before, through and after a handoff. Check without labels. |
| Give the voice facts a person can picture. | “An ocean away” prompted questions. The rewrite gave two named places, a distance, and identifiable people affected at home. | Replace “huge,” “tragic,” or “changed everything” with a person, consequence, quantity or time the reporting supports. Check the source, unit and date. |
| Give the audience a job during a hold. | A listening passage left about 2.44 seconds after a question. The Halifax memorial let the inscription and receding street sound finish the film. | Write “find the stamp,” “compare the two readings,” or “hear the second click” beside the hold. Time a first-time viewer doing it. |

## Build the middle and ending

The opening poses a question the next scene can answer. Move from the one thing the viewer can recognise to its wider cause. Return to the affected person or place while the earlier landmark is still recognisable. Choose an ending that changes the meaning of the opening object, reveals a comparison, or gives the viewer time to make a judgement. An unresolved question can work if the reporting genuinely leaves it open.

For a 45–60 second short, sketch a first pass with an event in the opening seconds, a clue and question in the first ten, an explanation or change of scale in the next ten, and a consequence before the return. Let new evidence determine the remaining beats. In a two-minute film, repeat the question-and-answer structure at chapter boundaries and give each new chapter a distinct visual task. A person should be able to tell you why the film moved from one scene to the next.

## Budget actual speech

In ten distinct study cuts the median was about **163 words per finished minute**, with a range of **128–186**. Within continuing runs of speech the range was approximately **185–236 WPM**. For the second number, count words from the first audible word to the last, removing internal gaps of at least 0.50 seconds. These are observations, with approximate word alignment. Two study identifiers referred to the same source and counted once.

A 50-second film at 150 finished WPM allows about 125 words including its visual holds. Alternatively reserve the viewing gaps first and budget just the speaking seconds at an appropriate within-run pace. Use one calculation. Speak it against the animatic. A rushed end of sentence, unexplained proper noun, or clue named before it can be seen is a reason to rewrite. The correct take can have different sentence lengths and a consistent human pace.
